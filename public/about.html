About Tarot

A centuries-old tradition. A symbolic language. A tool for reflection.

Origin and History

Tarot first appeared in 15th-century Europe as a card game — the Italian tarocchi — before becoming a tool for divination in the 18th century. The most influential decks, such as the Rider-Waite-Smith (1909), established the imagery that remains standard today.

Far from being an obscure practice, tarot has been studied by philosophers, psychologists and artists. Carl Jung saw the arcana as a representation of the archetypes of the collective unconscious.

The Structure of the Deck

A complete tarot deck has 78 cards, divided into two groups:

  • 22 Major Arcana — these represent universal forces, life themes and lessons of the soul. From The Fool (0) to The World (XXI).
  • 56 Minor Arcana — divided into four suits (Wands, Cups, Swords, Pentacles), each with 14 cards. They reflect everyday situations and emotional states.

How to Interpret

There is no single correct interpretation for a card. Meaning emerges from the interaction between the symbol, its position in the spread, and the experience of the person consulting.

The reader is the interpreter — the cards are the vocabulary. The same card can mean opposite things depending on the context, its orientation (upright or reversed), and the cards around it.
